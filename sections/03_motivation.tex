\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motiv}

Throughput numbers in this section are modelled on the mobile platform and the edge server that
Section~\ref{sec:method} details.

\subsection{PIM--Host Crossover}
\label{sec:bg:roofline}

Fig.~\ref{fig:roofline} plots skinny-GEMM throughput against $M$ in the roofline form~\cite{roofline} for the host
NPU's roof and for simulated PIM GEMMs of stock AiM and each platform's rival; each PIM curve is the geometric mean over
the FC shapes of the platform's models. Fig.~\ref{fig:roofline} and the later throughput figures plot $M = 2^k$; at
other $M$, PIM throughput dips by group quantization. The mobile NPU is the Snapdragon 8 Gen 3's~\cite{sd8g3}, which reaches
56\,GB/s~\cite{powerinfer2} of its own 68\,GB/s pins~\cite{heterollm}; applied to the 51.2\,GB/s pins of x64
LPDDR5-6400, that fraction gives the NPU 42\,GB/s\ASSUMPTION. The edge-server NPU is P3-LLM's~\cite{p3llm} at
256\,GB/s and 131\,TOPS\ASSUMPTION.

\paragraph{Host roof in the skinny range}
The host stays memory-bound across the whole skinny range. The 8 Gen 3~\cite{sd8g3} NPU reaches its compute roof only at
$M \approx 143$, and P3-LLM's~\cite{p3llm} NPU reaches its roof at $M = 128$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:roofline}a, b). Each NPU's
ridge lies far above $M = 32$, so every skinny GEMM runs on the host's memory roof, which rises linearly with $M$.

\paragraph{Stock-PIM crossover}
Stock PIM beats the host only for the first few token rows. At $M = 1$ every PIM ceiling starts 9.75$\times$ above the
mobile NPU's roof and 4$\times$ above the edge-server NPU's roof. Stock AiM's ceiling is flat from $M = 1$, however,
because stock AiM repeats the GEMV once per token row (Section~\ref{sec:bg:cmd}). The rising host roof overtakes that
ceiling, and stock AiM wins a whole decode step only up to $M = 6$ on the mobile platform and $M = 2$--3 on the edge
server, below most of the published values of $M$ (Section~\ref{sec:bg:skinny}).

\paragraph{GEMV-only use of PIM}
Stock PIM is a GEMV engine, and published systems keep PIM for GEMVs and send the GEMMs to the host.
AttAcc~\cite{attacc} runs batched FC layers on the xPU and attention on PIM. NeuPIMs~\cite{neupims} maps the GEMMs to
the NPU because PIM ``lacks the computational power to handle GEMM effectively.'' IANUS~\cite{ianus} chooses NPU or PIM
per FC layer. P3-LLM~\cite{p3llm} offloads its linear layers to the NPU from batch 8, where its PIM becomes
compute-bound.

\subsection{Command-Bound PIM Execution}
\label{sec:bg:cmd}

\paragraph{Command pacing}
In stock PIM a DRAM column command starts every MAC: HBM-PIM~\cite{hbmpim} executes one instruction per column
command, and AiM~\cite{aimjssc} clocks its PU at its interface's $t_\mathrm{CCD}$. In Fig.~\ref{fig:dram_pim}b each all-bank
MAC command starts one beat, and a PU clocked at the command clock CK finishes that beat in one CK cycle and waits for the next command.
Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline} follows one weight column of one bank through $M = 8$ tokens, in column periods $T$ with a PU
clock that fits $D = 4$ beats into each period; the first three rows of Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline} show the same pacing in
the prior designs of Section~\ref{sec:bg:walls}. The column commands and their spacing,
\tccds{} and \tccdl, exist to access the DRAM array: each column command drives the bank's column decoder and I/O sense
amplifiers to move one column out of the cell array~\cite{hbmpim}. A MAC that executes only when a column command
arrives therefore inherits array-access timing as the limit of its throughput. A faster-PU test confirms that the
command count, not the PU's arithmetic, sets the time of a stock GEMM. Stock AiM repeats the GEMV once per token row,
so its MAC command count equals $M$ times the number of weight columns, and in our simulation its GEMM time grows in
proportion to $M$, within 1.84\,\% on the mobile platform and 2.7\,\% on the edge server. Replacing the column-matched
stock PU of Table~\ref{tab:params}, one beat per column period, with a PU four times faster, clocked at CK (0.8\,GHz on
LPDDR5-6400 and 1\,GHz on HBM2), leaves every stock GEMM at the same number of cycles, because the command spacing, not
the PU clock, sets the time of each MAC command's slot (Fig.~\ref{fig:dram_pim}b).

\paragraph{Time breakdown}
Useful MAC work fills at most a fifth of a stock GEMM's time. Fig.~\ref{fig:breakdown} splits stock AiM's GEMM time at $M = 8$ into
MAC slots and the non-MAC commands between them, on one channel of each platform (channel 28 of the mobile platform and
channel 30 of the edge server), for two kernels: one tile, whose weights fill one row sweep in each bank and whose
inputs fill one GB chunk ($K \times N$ of $2048 \times 64$ on the mobile platform and $4096 \times 256$ on the edge
server), and the $4096 \times 4096$ query projection (q\_proj) of Llama-2-7B on the mobile platform and of Llama-3.1-8B
on the edge server. The MAC slots hold useful beats and idle beats; the non-MAC commands are the row-switch commands,
the token load/drain commands and refresh. The useful share of the time is the useful-MAC utilization
$U = W \tck / T_\mathrm{GEMM}$, where $W$ is the number of useful MAC beats per PU, $T_\mathrm{GEMM}$ is the GEMM time
and the reference PU runs one useful beat per CK cycle; the cycle \tck is 1.25\,ns on the mobile platform and 1\,ns on
the edge server, and the reference PU fits $D = 4$ beats into each column period. Across the four kernels, useful beats
take 14.1--19.1\,\% of the time, idle beats 42.2--57.4\,\% and the non-MAC commands 23.4--43.7\,\%; the bars leave out
the remaining stalls, at most 0.015\,\%. Stock AiM thus loses useful MAC beats to two bottlenecks: idle beats inside
the MAC slots, and non-MAC commands between them.

\paragraph{Idle beats}
Idle beats fill three quarters of every stock MAC slot. $U$ is the product of a slot factor and a sweep factor,
$U = \UMAC \times \fMAC$. The slot factor $\UMAC = A\,b\,\tck / \tslot$ is the useful share of one MAC command's slot
\tslot: the slot serves $A$ tokens with $b$ beats each ($b = 1$ on the mobile platform; $b = 2$ on the edge server,
where each MAC command feeds a PU from its two banks). Stock AiM's slot serves one token, so the command pacing of
Fig.~\ref{fig:dram_pim}b leaves three of the slot's four beats idle, and stock AiM's \UMAC is 1/4. Idle beats
therefore take three times the time of the useful beats and form the largest segment of every bar in
Fig.~\ref{fig:breakdown}: 53.9\,\% of q\_proj's time on the mobile platform and 57.4\,\% on the edge server.

\paragraph{Non-MAC commands}
Stock AiM's row-switch commands outlast the useful MAC work of their row sweep. The sweep factor \fMAC is the share of
the GEMM time spent in MAC slots rather than in the non-MAC commands of the row sweeps (Fig.~\ref{fig:dram_pim}c).
Each row sweep of $c$ MAC slots pays the \emph{row-switch overhead} \trow once, for its \emph{row-switch commands}: the
two mode switches (TMOD), the row's PREA and ACT16 pair and the WRMAC16. Each row sweep pays \ttok once per token, for the \emph{token load/drain
commands}, the token's share of WRGB and its RDMAC16 drain:
\begin{equation}
  \fMAC = \frac{(1 - \rho)\, c\, \tslot}{c\, \tslot + \trow + A\, \ttok},
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the share of time that refresh takes. Fitted on stock AiM at $M = 1$, \trow is 87.73\,ns and \ttok
4.26\,ns with $\rho = 0.070$ on the mobile platform, and \trow is
94.76\,ns and \ttok 9.64\,ns with $\rho = 0.061$ on the edge server. Over one decoder layer's weight GEMMs on the mobile platform, where
each stock row sweep serves one token, the simulated row-switch commands take 91.2\,ns per sweep, against 78.0\,ns of
useful MAC work, the time $c\,b\,\tck$ that the reference PU needs for the sweep's beats. The two TMODs take the
largest part, 40.5\,ns, and the ACT16 pair, timed by a simulator preset, takes 37.9\,ns. PREA takes 11.5\,ns and
WRMAC16 1.2\,ns. The fitted \trow of 87.73\,ns lies below the sum because the fit nets out the MAC tail that overlaps
the row-switch commands. In Fig.~\ref{fig:breakdown}, the row-switch commands take 19.5\,\% of q\_proj's time on the
mobile platform and 13.8\,\% on the edge server. The token load/drain commands scale with the tokens and the GB fills
instead. RDMAC16 drains each token's sums once per row sweep and takes at most 1.0\,\% of a kernel's time. WRGB fills
the GB once per token and $K$ chunk, and one fill serves every row sweep over that chunk: 64 row sweeps in the mobile
q\_proj, 16 in the edge-server q\_proj and one in either one-tile kernel. Token load therefore takes 0.6\,\% and
2.4\,\% of q\_proj's time but 28.3\,\% and 28.5\,\% of the one tile's. Refresh takes 7.1\,\% of q\_proj's time on the mobile platform and
6.2\,\% on the edge server. On the edge server, stock AiM's non-MAC time over the layer, 146.2\,ns per row sweep
including 41.8\,ns of refresh, exceeds its 123.3\,ns of useful MAC work. Over one decoder layer's weight GEMMs, stock AiM's MAC slots fill
$\fMAC = 0.717$ of the time on the mobile platform, so stock AiM's $U$ is $1/4 \times 0.717 = 0.179$
(Table~\ref{tab:umac}); on the edge server, stock AiM's $U$ is 0.193. Section~\ref{sec:bg:depth} names one lever for
each of the two bottlenecks.

\subsection{GEMM Support in Prior PIM}
\label{sec:bg:walls}

Prior designs raise the PIM ceiling in two families, and each family still paces every MAC by a command.

\paragraph{Wider-PU family}
Six designs widen the MAC step with multipliers. LP-Spec~\cite{lpspec} builds its matrix unit from four 32-wide INT8
ALUs, clocks the ALUs at 200\,MHz to match the memory's 5\,ns $t_\mathrm{CCD}$, and shares one unit between two banks;
we model one such unit per bank. One column command feeds the four ALUs with four tokens, one MAC step each, so the 8
tokens take 2 periods, 2 commands and 2 reads, on four times the stock multipliers. The 4$\times$ ceiling moves the
crossover of this per-bank design with the host to $M = 20$--24 on the mobile platform (Fig.~\ref{fig:roofline}a).
PAPI~\cite{papi} places four processing units in each bank, and StepStone~\cite{stepstone} places SIMD units at the
bank groups or beside the devices of a memory module. Duplex~\cite{duplex} and H$^2$-LLM~\cite{h2llm} build their
multipliers on a logic die under the DRAM, and MoNDE~\cite{monde} builds them into a memory-side controller. We take
LP-Spec~\cite{lpspec} as the representative of the wider-PU family and model it as an \textbf{LP-Spec-style} design,
the rival on the mobile platform (Section~\ref{sec:eval:phone}). LP-Spec-style's slot serves four tokens, so
LP-Spec-style reaches $\UMAC = 1$ (Table~\ref{tab:umac}) only with four times the multipliers: per multiplier,
LP-Spec-style's $U$ is 0.173--0.174 on the mobile platform and 0.185 on the edge server, below stock AiM's.

\paragraph{Faster-PU family}
A faster PU lets at most two tokens share one column read. P3-LLM~\cite{p3llm} keeps the weight of a column read
latched in its PU, issues one further command per token and clocks its PU at 500\,MHz, one beat per HBM2 \tccds.
Commands may issue only every \tccds, ``typically a half of \tccdl{}''~\cite{p3llm}, while the all-bank column runs at
\tccdl~\cite{hbmpim}, so at most two tokens share a read: the 8 tokens take 4 periods, 8 commands and 4 reads. On
every memory family whose \tccdl{} is twice \tccds{}, the depth cap is 2, so the faster-PU family's slot serves at
most two tokens and its \UMAC stops at 1/2. The 2$\times$ ceiling moves the crossover of
this design with the host to $M = 4$--6 on the edge server (Fig.~\ref{fig:roofline}b). P3-LLM~\cite{p3llm} is the only
design that applies a faster PU to $M > 1$, so we take P3-LLM as the representative of the faster-PU family and model
it as a \textbf{P3-LLM-style} design, the rival on the edge server (Section~\ref{sec:eval:server}).

\paragraph{Cost of logic in a DRAM die}
Logic in a DRAM die costs area: a DRAM process builds logic 10$\times$ less dense than an ASIC process and routes the
logic in three metal layers instead of ten or more~\cite{devaux}. In GDDR6-AiM~\cite{aimjssc} silicon, one PU occupies
0.19\,mm$^2$. Samsung's HBM-PIM~\cite{hbmpim} halves its DRAM sub-arrays to make room for its PUs, and the PU-based
prototypes lose 50\,\% of memory capacity~\cite{pimpal}. By LP-Spec's~\cite{lpspec} own estimate its matrix units take
16.92\,\% of a 16\,Gb LPDDR5 die, against 20--25\,\% of the chip for all of AiM's~\cite{aimjssc} compute circuits.
Four times the multipliers per bank therefore cost capacity, and per unit of logic area the order of the two families
reverses: LP-Spec-style's ceiling per logic mm$^2$, 314\,GOPS on the mobile platform and 697 on the edge server, falls
below P3-LLM-style's 390 and 830. Logic-die designs build their multipliers in a logic process on a separate
die~\cite{duplex,h2llm}. Hybrid bonding, one way to attach that die, brings an integration overhead of its
own~\cite{h2llm}.

The remaining LLM PIM designs fall outside the wider-PU and faster-PU families. GEMV-only designs serve one input
vector per weight read~\cite{newton,cent,pimgpt}, lookup-table designs replace multiplications with table
lookups~\cite{localut,pimpal,pimdl}, and scheduling and dataflow designs reorganize the work around the
PU~\cite{pimba,paise,splitsync,dots,pimphony}. Paik et al.~\cite{paik2022} decouple memory access from computation
across banks so that per-bank PIM reaches all-bank throughput on a standard interface. Three designs, the closest prior
art, run several MAC beats per column read, yet each design holds one input vector ($M = 1$): SAL-PIM~\cite{salpim}
clocks its MAC units at twice the column rate, the Precision-Flexible PU~\cite{pfpu} runs four phases per column
interval on packed low-bit operands, and CD-PIM~\cite{cdpim} runs two compute units per bank at twice the internal
clock. CD-PIM~\cite{cdpim} leaves the GEMM to the host processor, and the extra beats of all three designs work on the
same vector.

\subsection{The Opportunity: DRAM Timing Bounds Access, Not MACs}
\label{sec:bg:depth}

The column commands of Sections~\ref{sec:bg:cmd} and~\ref{sec:bg:walls} bound how often a column leaves the array, not
how often a PU can compute. The PU takes its operands from the I/O sense amplifiers at the bank's I/O
boundary~\cite{hbmpim}, so a column the PU has latched stays usable after its command: P3-LLM~\cite{p3llm} reuses a
latched column for two tokens at unchanged cell-access and column-decoder power, the majority of PIM power. The column
period $t_\mathrm{col}$ thus sets how often weights arrive at a PU, and the PU clock $f_\mathrm{PU}$ sets how often the
PU computes on the weights. The product $D = \lfloor t_\mathrm{col} \times f_\mathrm{PU} \rfloor$ is the number of
beats one column read can feed. A column lasts 5\,ns on LPDDR5-6400~\cite{lpspec,ramulator2} and \tccdl${}=4$\,ns on
HBM2~\cite{hbmpim,p3llm}. Each period lasts four cycles of CK, \tck${}= 1.25$\,ns on LPDDR5-6400
and 1\,ns on HBM2~\cite{hbmpim,p3llm}, so a PU clocked at CK, 0.8\,GHz on LPDDR5-6400 and 1\,GHz on HBM2, fits
$D = 4$ beats into either period. HBM3 at 6.4\,Gb/s leaves a 2.5\,ns
column~\cite{ramulator2,p3llm}, so a 1\,GHz PU gets $D = 2$ there, the same depth P3-LLM-style reaches with commands.

\paragraph{PU clock in a DRAM die}
A PU clocked at CK has precedent in DRAM silicon and in a PIM design for HBM2. HBM2's DRAM core runs at a quarter of
the 1.0--1.2\,GHz bus clock, and Samsung's HBM-PIM~\cite{hbmpim} sets its FP16 PU to the core frequency,
250--300\,MHz, in a 20\,nm DRAM process. Logic in later DRAM processes runs faster: UPMEM's processor~\cite{devaux}
executes 32-bit integer instructions at 500\,MHz in a 2x-nm DRAM process, and GDDR6-AiM~\cite{aimjssc} runs BF16
multipliers and adder trees at 1\,GHz in a 1y-nm DRAM process. The 1y-nm process also builds HBM: Samsung's
HBM2E~\cite{flashbolt} stacks eight 1y-nm DRAM dies. The Precision-Flexible PU~\cite{pfpu} clocks an HBM-PIM-style
PU at \tck, 1\,GHz on HBM2, and splits each 4-\tck column interval into four phases.

At $D = 4$, stock AiM's one beat per command lets a PU compute one beat of four per column, for $\UMAC = 1/4$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dram_pim}b), and P3-LLM-style's commands let it compute two, for $\UMAC = 1/2$. Two levers remain,
one for each bottleneck of Section~\ref{sec:bg:cmd}: \emph{beat filling} uses all four beats of a slot without
adding multipliers, which raises the slot factor, and \emph{row reuse} lets the $A$ tokens of a row sweep reuse its
open row and pay its row-switch commands once, which raises the sweep factor. A token then pays $\trow / A + \ttok$ of
non-MAC commands per row sweep, plus its share of refresh. With refresh included, the simulated non-MAC time per token
falls from 123.39\,ns at $A = 1$ to 22.15\,ns at $A = 8$ on the mobile platform (Table~\ref{tab:umac}) and is 146.17,
78.70 and 43.51\,ns at $A = 1$, 2 and 4 on the edge server. Table~\ref{tab:umac} prints simulated values; on the designs the formula was not fitted on, the formula's $U$
lies within 0.33\,\% of the simulated $U$ on the mobile platform and within 0.59\,\% on the edge server. Sections~\ref{sec:design:beat} and~\ref{sec:design:reuse} build both levers into CoDe, whose MACs follow
the PU clock: with one PU, one command and the stock multiplier count, CoDe's ceiling reaches 583 and 1,247\,GOPS per logic
mm$^2$, and CoDe's $U$ reaches 0.779 on the mobile platform (Table~\ref{tab:umac}).
